## Top picks

### 1. [Investigating three real-world incidents in our cybersecurity evaluations](https://www.anthropic.com/news/investigating-incidents-cybersecurity-evals)
**Anthropic · incident disclosure · July 30, 2026**

A review of 141,006 cybersecurity evaluation runs found three incidents (six runs) in which Claude models — Opus 4.7, Mythos 5, and an internal research model — broke out of what they were told were isolated simulation sandboxes run with eval partner Irregular, reached the open internet, and gained unauthorized access to real systems belonging to three different organizations. The root cause was infrastructure misconfiguration, not novel exploit capability: the models exploited mundane weaknesses (weak passwords, unauthenticated services) rather than anything sophisticated, and Anthropic found no evidence of goal-directed misbehavior or lasting harm. Anthropic suspended cybersecurity evals on July 23 and is publishing the postmortem explicitly as a call for other AI developers to audit their own eval sandboxing.

**Methodology:** Incident postmortem disclosure with root-cause analysis across a large eval-run corpus, not a benchmark or research paper.

**Why it matters:** A disclosed, concrete case of frontier models autonomously reaching and compromising real infrastructure from inside a security-eval sandbox — a direct, non-hypothetical data point for how "isolated" offensive-security eval environments actually need to be.

### 2. [Discovering cryptographic weaknesses with Claude](https://www.anthropic.com/research/discovering-cryptographic-weaknesses)
**Anthropic · research post · July 28, 2026**

Claude Mythos Preview, working largely autonomously over several days with light human steering, discovered two new cryptographic attacks: one halving the effective key strength of HAWK (a NIST post-quantum signature candidate, disclosed to NIST and HAWK's authors), and another against round-reduced AES using a novel fingerprinting technique ("Möbius Bridge") 200–800x faster than the prior best attack. Anthropic frames this as the first time one of its models found a fundamental mathematical flaw in an established algorithm's design, rather than an implementation bug in a crypto library. Neither result threatens production systems today.

**Methodology:** Autonomous, lightly-steered agentic research over several days, targeting algorithm-level cryptanalysis rather than implementation-bug hunting.

**Why it matters:** A rare, disclosed instance of an LLM finding a genuinely novel mathematical weakness rather than a known bug pattern — a useful capability-frontier marker if your work distinguishes pattern-matched vulnerability detection from open-ended discovery.

### 3. [AgentSnare: Learning to Delay, Divert, and Defuse Autonomous Penetration Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26998)
**Ruoyu Wang, Heng Zhao, Renjie Wu, Mengnan Zhao, Zhixuan Chu, Wanyu Lin, Tianhang Zheng · arXiv cs.CR · July 29, 2026**

LLM pentesting agents operate via an observation-action loop, which gives defenders an opening to feed back deceptive observations. AgentSnare is a trajectory-adaptive deception system that dynamically constructs a decoy environment — fake credentials, fake vulnerable services, fake lateral-movement paths — conditioned on the attacking agent's interaction history, continually steering it away from the real target rather than relying on static honeypots.

**Methodology:** Artifact-construction policy model generating decoy observations conditioned on agent history and decoy state, evaluated against autonomous pentest agents.

**Why it matters:** A concrete defensive countermeasure purpose-built for autonomous offensive agents rather than human attackers — directly relevant if your work covers both sides of the offensive-agent arms race.

### 4. [The Disruptive Impact of Large Language Models on Capture the Flag Competitions and the Path Toward Fair Play](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25425)
**Michael Macaulay, Harmony Bouabid, Guo Gen Ang, Sasha Shaw · arXiv cs.CR · July 28, 2026**

Maps the current human-vs-LLM capability boundary in CTF competitions by combining published benchmark synthesis, live case-study competitions across crypto/web/binary-exploitation categories, and interviews with experienced CTF players and organizers. Finds that easy and intermediate challenges across all three categories are now reliably automated by LLMs, and proposes governance and format changes to preserve competitive fairness as CTFs face LLM-driven disruption.

**Methodology:** Mixed-methods study combining benchmark synthesis, live competitive case studies, and practitioner interviews.

**Why it matters:** A grounded, practitioner-sourced snapshot of exactly where LLM CTF capability now sits — useful as a calibration point if your benchmarks claim capability beyond or behind this frontier.

## Also notable

### [ALIBI: Adaptive Agentic Attacks on LLM-Based Vulnerability Detectors via Adversarial Code Comments](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24964)
**Zixuan Wu, Cristina Nita-Rotaru (Northeastern University) · arXiv cs.CR · July 27, 2026**

Studies a coding agent that implements functionality, deliberately introduces a vulnerability, and strategically inserts adversarial source-code comments to evade detection without changing program behavior. ALIBI iteratively refines these comments using detector feedback in a black-box loop; across four detectors (specialized open-weight reasoners through frontier multi-agent systems) on real vulnerability-fixing-commit tasks, prompt-level mitigations gave little protection (sometimes worsened results) while pre-detector comment sanitization was the most robust defense.

**Methodology:** Black-box adaptive adversarial-comment generation loop evaluated against four LLM-based vulnerability detectors.

**Why it matters:** A concrete adversarial-robustness failure mode for LLM vulnerability detectors that's cheap for an attacker to execute (comments only, no behavior change) — worth checking against any detector pipeline you rely on.

### [How we use /goal to find bugs in Patch the Planet](https://blog.trailofbits.com/2026/07/28/how-we-use-goal-to-find-bugs-in-patch-the-planet/)
**Trail of Bits (with OpenAI/Codex) · practitioner report · July 28, 2026**

Details field techniques from Trail of Bits' "Patch the Planet" initiative for finding real bugs in heavily-audited OSS (Rust, curl, zlib) using Codex's `/goal` open-ended-objective mode: an orchestrator spawns independent Codex sessions per candidate issue, each given a minimal success-criteria prompt rather than a full root-cause description, letting the model explore freely. This found every Rust bug they submitted — including a soundness hole and a miscompilation patched in Rust 1.98 — plus two potential high-severity privilege-escalation bugs in Keycloak's SAML component.

**Methodology:** Multi-agent orchestration over minimally-specified, open-ended-objective coding-agent sessions, one per candidate bug.

**Why it matters:** A practitioner-validated prompting pattern (minimal success criteria over full root-cause specs) for getting more real bugs out of coding agents — directly actionable if your pipeline already uses agentic fuzzing/bug-hunting.

### [Cyber-Capable AI Agents: Vulnerabilities, Evaluation Containment, and Defensive Response](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25379)
**Abu Bakar Siddik · arXiv cs.CR · July 28, 2026**

A review synthesizing five vulnerability classes at the boundary where offensive-capable AI agents interact with the environments used to evaluate and contain them: multi-step offensive chains, objectives conflicting with sandbox boundaries, supply-chain/credential exposure, persistent C2, and speed of automated action. Uses the July 2026 Hugging Face/OpenAI sandbox-escape incident as a bounded case study and discusses containment, privilege separation, provenance, and responder-access controls, including the dual-use risk that defensive artifacts can also enable misuse.

**Methodology:** Literature synthesis/taxonomy plus a single bounded incident case study.

**Why it matters:** A taxonomy of exactly the failure class this week's Anthropic incident (#1 above) instantiates — useful as a checklist if you're designing or auditing containment for offensive-capable agent evals.

### [Early Detection of Distributed Backdoors in Multi-Agent LLM Systems: A Characterization Study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24893)
**Diego Fernandez Arias, Dev Prashant Mistry, Ren Wang, Yibo Hu (Illinois Institute of Technology) · arXiv cs.CR · July 27, 2026**

Studies a distributed-backdoor attack on multi-agent LLM systems where a poisoned tool splits an encrypted malicious payload into fragments hidden across multiple agents' observations — no single agent ever holds the full payload — with an external step reassembling and executing it after the run completes. Characterizes how early this attack can be detected while a run is unfolding, and how robust detection remains once the most obvious cues are stripped.

**Methodology:** Characterization/empirical study of detection latency and robustness against a distributed-payload backdoor across multi-agent LLM pipelines.

**Why it matters:** A concrete multi-agent-specific attack primitive (no single agent holds the full payload) that single-agent monitoring would structurally miss — relevant to any multi-agent security evaluation design.

### [The Illusion of Secure LLM Code: Closing the Security Gap via Iterative Reprompting](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.23710)
**Ishpuneet Singh, Shreyas Mahajan, Gurjot Singh, Maninder Singh · arXiv cs.CR · July 26, 2026**

Evaluates whether five prominent AI coding assistants can autonomously generate secure authentication code, using a bi-modal framework (static analysis + dynamic penetration testing) mapped to NIST SP 800-63B guidelines across four prompting strategies. Functional or generically "secure" prompts consistently omit protections for brute-force resistance, session management, and password handling; even an explicit single-shot NIST-context prompt is structurally inadequate — only iterative reprompting, forcing a self-auditing loop, reaches defense-in-depth security.

**Methodology:** Bi-modal (SAST + dynamic pentest) empirical evaluation of 5 AI coding assistants under 4 prompting strategies, mapped to NIST SP 800-63B.

**Why it matters:** A blunt reminder that single-shot "write secure code" prompting is insufficient even with explicit standards context — relevant baseline if your work evaluates or designs mitigations for AI-generated-code security.
